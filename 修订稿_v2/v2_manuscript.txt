H1|Abstract
P|**Objective.** To quantify how much prognostic information is added by progression status and by the timing of first progression after first-line treatment of advanced serous tubo-ovarian carcinoma, and to explain why the two can appear to differ in value.
P|**Methods.** We analysed public individual-patient data from eight cohorts with stage III–IV serous carcinoma. In four cohorts with age, stage and residual disease (TCGA, AOCS, UKE and the ICON7 tissue subset), censoring-weighted logistic models predicted death within 12 months of a month-12 landmark. The prespecified analysis locked equations fitted in TCGA. Post hoc analyses used internal–external cross-validation with random-effects summaries, landmarks from 6 to 24 months, an illness–death model, post-progression survival in all patients with recorded progression, tumour expression scores, hallmark enrichment with single-cell attribution, and BRCA1/2, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) and CCNE1 status in TCGA.
P|**Results.** At month 12 (850 patients, 142 deaths), progression status raised the held-out AUC over age, stage and residual disease by 0.180 (95% CI, 0.095 to 0.265), whereas time since progression changed it by −0.000 (−0.004 to 0.004). Only 19 of 221 progressed patients had progressed more than six months earlier. With later landmarks the pooled increment from timing rose to 0.071 (0.006 to 0.136) at month 24. Among 1,126 patients followed from first progression (726 deaths), each six months of later progression was associated with a pooled adjusted hazard ratio for death of 0.73 (0.66 to 0.82), and concordance in held-out cohorts rose by 0.088 (0.047 to 0.130). Observed-to-expected mortality for held-out cohorts ranged from 0.67 to 1.85. A T-cell expression score remained associated with death after adjustment for progression status (hazard ratio per SD, 0.86; 0.75 to 0.98) but added 0.007 (−0.012 to 0.026) to the AUC. Epithelial–mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis programmes were enriched in tumours that progressed within 12 months in two independent cohort sets. Single-cell data attributed these genes mainly to fibroblasts, and the enrichment disappeared after adjustment for a stromal score. BRCA1/2 mutation, HRD score and CCNE1 amplification were associated with long-term but not 24-month mortality after the landmark.
P|**Conclusions.** Progression timing is strongly prognostic, but a month-12 landmark observes too narrow a range of it to improve prediction beyond progression status. Predicted probabilities required local recalibration. Baseline expression and genomic features added little to short-term prediction once progression status was known.
P|**Keywords.** Serous tubo-ovarian carcinoma, disease progression, landmark analysis, multistate model, post-progression survival, external validation, gene expression.
H1|Introduction
P|Prognosis after surgery for advanced serous tubo-ovarian carcinoma is usually described with information available at diagnosis, such as stage and residual disease [1]. That estimate ages quickly. One year later some patients have relapsed and others have not, and clinicians already act on this distinction. The interval from the end of platinum treatment to relapse has long been used to describe prognosis and to choose second-line therapy, although consensus statements now discourage rigid six-month categories [2, 3].
P|Several recent studies have quantified updated prognosis. Su and colleagues showed that progression-free status at 9, 12, 15 and 18 months after the start of first-line chemotherapy was associated with subsequent overall survival in 487 patients with BRCA wild-type disease [4]. Conditional survival estimates describe how prognosis changes with time survived or time free of progression [5]. A validated nomogram uses time to recurrence for patients who are already at relapse [6], and early CA-125 dynamics carry prognostic information during first-line treatment [7]. These studies leave two practical questions open. When a patient is seen at a fixed visit and her progression status is known, does the date of progression add anything to the prediction of death over the next year? And if the answer depends on when the visit takes place, why?
P|We addressed these questions in public individual-patient data. We first compared four nested sets of predictors at a month-12 landmark and tested how well the resulting risks transported between cohorts. We then examined the same question across landmarks from 6 to 24 months, in an illness–death model and in all patients followed from first progression. Because every cohort was assembled for tumour expression profiling, we also asked whether baseline expression scores and genomic features retained prognostic value once progression status was known, which transcriptional programmes distinguished tumours that progressed within 12 months, and which cell types express them.
H1|Methods
H2|Study design and reporting
P|This was a retrospective prognostic study of de-identified public data, reported according to TRIPOD+AI [8] (Supplementary Table S1). The study was not registered. An analysis plan was written after source fields and follow-up had been inspected and before any model was fitted. It specified the month-12 landmark, the 12-month horizon, three nested models fitted in TCGA and their unchanged application to three other cohorts. All other analyses were added afterwards in response to internal statistical, clinical and pathology review. Supplementary Table S2 labels every analysis as prespecified or post hoc.
H2|Cohorts and participants
P|Eligible patients were adults with source-recorded stage III–IV serous ovarian, fallopian tube or primary peritoneal carcinoma and valid overall survival and progression fields. The four primary cohorts recorded age, stage and residual disease: TCGA [9, 10], AOCS [11], UKE [12] and the public AGO-OVAR11 tissue subset of ICON7 (GSE140082) [13, 14]. Four further cohorts contributed to analyses that did not need the full set of baseline predictors: GSE32062 (Japan), GSE30161 (UVA), GSE26193 (Curie) and GSE49997 (OVCAD) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. AOCS patients were required to have had primary surgery without recorded neoadjuvant chemotherapy and to have received platinum. Recurrent-tumour specimens were excluded in UKE and duplicate specimens were collapsed to one record per patient. Table 1 gives the time origin and progression definition of each primary cohort.
P|Histological grade was not harmonised. TCGA, AOCS and Japan recorded historical three-tier grades, UKE recorded none, and the ICON7 subset carried a two-level source grade in which 59 of 250 graded eligible records were labelled low grade. That proportion is far above the expected frequency of low-grade serous carcinoma, so we treated the field as a historical grade proxy and used it only in sensitivity analyses. UKE, OVCAD and the ICON7 subset all recruited in German-speaking centres, and no shared identifier allows overlap to be excluded.
H2|Predictors and outcome at the landmark
P|The landmark was 12 months after each source's time origin, which was diagnosis in TCGA, AOCS and UKE and randomisation in ICON7. Patients entered the risk set if they were alive and under observation after the landmark and their progression status at the landmark was known. The outcome was death from any cause within the following 12 months. The Baseline model contained age per 10 years, stage IV versus III and residual disease greater than 1 cm (or the source's suboptimal category). Progression-only contained a binary indicator of recorded progression by the landmark. Status combined both. Timing added the months since first progression, centred at six and divided by six, and set to zero in patients without progression.
P|Models were logistic regressions weighted by the inverse probability of censoring, with a weak ridge penalty (inverse strength 100). Censoring distributions were estimated by Kaplan–Meier within each cohort [20, 21, 22]. Performance measures were the cumulative/dynamic AUC, the Brier score, the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio and the calibration slope.
H2|Development and validation strategies
P|The prespecified strategy fitted the equations in TCGA and applied them unchanged to AOCS, UKE and ICON7. Review of that analysis showed that the TCGA equation gave stage IV a negative coefficient and that the Baseline model discriminated poorly elsewhere. We therefore added internal–external cross-validation, in which each of the four cohorts was held out in turn and the models were fitted in the other three [23, 24]. Held-out estimates were combined with DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models with Hartung–Knapp intervals, and we report 95% prediction intervals for a new cohort [25, 26]. Confidence intervals for cohort-level estimates used 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the held-out cohort. A sensitivity analysis removed the other German cohort from training when UKE or ICON7 was held out.
P|For the TCGA-only equation we estimated, within each external cohort, the effect of updating the intercept alone and of refitting intercept and slope, using 20 repeats of five-fold cross-validation so that updating and evaluation used different patients. Smoothed calibration curves used a censoring-weighted natural spline of the linear predictor, summarised by the integrated calibration index [27]. Missing residual-disease status (39 TCGA, 32 AOCS and 2 ICON7 landmark patients) was multiply imputed 30 times from age, stage, progression status and outcome. We compared the available numbers of events with those needed for precise external validation [28, 29]. The eight machine-learning algorithms, penalisation diagnostics, nomogram and decision curves of the first draft were removed because the five predictors were fixed in advance and no clinical decision threshold had been defined.
H2|Timing across landmarks and disease states
P|To examine whether the result at month 12 depended on the landmark, we built risk sets at 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 months. A landmark supermodel stacked these risk sets and fitted a Cox model for death within 12 months, stratified by cohort and landmark, with robust standard errors clustered on the patient [20]. Internal–external AUC contrasts were repeated at each landmark. An illness–death model on the original time scale treated progression as a time-dependent state with delayed entry and tested whether the time of progression modified the hazard of death after progression [30]. Finally, all patients with a recorded progression were followed from the date of progression. Patients whose progression date coincided with death or last contact were excluded. Cox models for post-progression survival were adjusted for age, stage and residual disease and stratified by cohort (one-stage), or fitted by cohort with the available adjusters and pooled (two-stage). The shape of the association was examined with a natural cubic spline and with six-month categories. Gain in Harrell's concordance from adding time to progression was estimated in held-out cohorts.
P|As an illustration of use in trial design, we computed the number of patients needed to observe the 247 deaths required to detect a hazard ratio of 0.70 with 80% power over 12 months of follow-up in different landmark populations [31].
H2|Molecular analyses
P|Tumour expression profiles were available for 1,609 eligible patients in the eight cohorts (microarray in seven and RNA sequencing in UKE). Before any outcome analysis we specified three gene sets from genes characteristic of the stromal-response, immunoreactive and proliferative expression subtypes [11, 32]: 22 fibroblast and extracellular-matrix genes, 14 T-cell and cytotoxicity genes and 12 proliferation genes (Supplementary Table S3). Each score was the mean of within-cohort gene z-scores, rescaled to unit standard deviation within cohort. Cohort-specific Cox models related each score to progression-free survival, overall survival, death within 24 months of the month-12 landmark with and without adjustment for progression status, and post-progression survival, and were pooled by random effects. Incremental AUC from adding the scores to the Status model was estimated by internal–external cross-validation in the primary cohorts.
P|Germline and somatic BRCA1/2 mutations of the TCGA marker study were obtained from cBioPortal for sequenced cases [32, 33]. HRD scores, the sum of loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic imbalance and large-scale state transitions, were taken from the TCGA pan-cancer analysis of DNA damage repair [34] and analysed per 10 points and at the published threshold of 42 [35]. CCNE1 amplification (high-level gain by GISTIC), aneuploidy score, fraction of genome altered and nonsynonymous mutation burden came from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas release in cBioPortal [33]. Each feature was related to progression-free and overall survival, death after the month-12 landmark with adjustment for progression status, and post-progression survival, and its out-of-fold AUC increment over the Status model was estimated. For the transcriptome-wide comparison, tumours with recorded progression before 12 months were compared with tumours progression-free at 12 months by Welch tests within cohort. Gene-level z-scores were combined across cohorts by weighted Stouffer's method, separately in the four primary and the four additional cohorts. Pre-ranked gene set enrichment used the 50 MSigDB hallmark sets [36, 37]. A hallmark was called replicated if its false discovery rate was below 0.05 in the primary cohorts and it was nominally significant in the same direction in the additional cohorts.
P|To attribute the bulk signal to cell types we used single-cell RNA sequencing of metastatic tumour from 11 patients with high-grade serous carcinoma (GSE165897) [38]. Counts of treatment-naive cells were summed by patient and cell class (malignant, fibroblast, mesothelial, myeloid, T/NK and B/plasma), converted to log counts per million and averaged across patients. Classes needed at least 20 cells per patient in at least three patients (21,671 cells), which excluded endothelial cells. Specificity was the z-score of a gene across the six classes. Gene sets were compared with 5,000 random sets of equal size drawn from genes measured in all bulk cohorts and expressed in the single-cell data. The bulk comparison was then repeated with the stromal score as a covariate in gene-wise linear models.
P|Two earlier supplementary analyses are retained only in Supplementary Table S4: a slide-level stromal percentage from TCGA biospecimen quality control and an application of the TCGA equation to the APOLLO-2 cohort [39, 40]. Analyses used Python 3.12 with lifelines 0.30, scikit-learn 1.9 and gseapy.
H1|Results
H2|Cohorts and mortality at the landmark
P|The month-12 risk sets contained 347 TCGA, 133 AOCS, 146 UKE and 224 ICON7 patients, of whom 142 died within the next 12 months (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S5). Progression had been recorded by month 12 in 15.8% to 32.3% of patients. Estimated 12-month mortality was 33.5%, 59.0%, 63.0% and 49.3% in patients with progression and 7.2%, 5.8%, 12.3% and 6.4% in those without (Figure 1). The hazard ratio for progression status was larger in the first 12 months after the landmark (5.2, 16.2, 8.5 and 9.7) than in months 12 to 24 (3.5, 4.0, 6.9 and 5.0), which is consistent with the departure from proportional hazards detected in ICON7. The fixed-horizon logistic models do not rely on proportional hazards.
H2|Discrimination and calibration at month 12
P|In internal–external cross-validation the Status model reached AUCs of 0.761, 0.881, 0.793 and 0.851 in held-out TCGA, AOCS, UKE and ICON7 (Table 2). Adding progression status to the Baseline model raised the AUC by 0.180 (95% CI, 0.095 to 0.265; 95% prediction interval, 0.065 to 0.295). The Baseline model alone had a pooled AUC of 0.63 (0.54 to 0.72). Read in the other direction, adding age, stage and residual disease to progression status raised the AUC by 0.063 (0.021 to 0.106), without a clear change in Brier score (−0.004; −0.012 to 0.005). Adding time since progression changed the AUC by −0.000 (−0.004 to 0.004) (Figure 2A and 2B).
P|The prespecified TCGA-only equation gave similar discrimination but a weaker Baseline model (AUC 0.636, 0.524 and 0.672 in AOCS, UKE and ICON7) and a stage IV coefficient of −0.86 (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). Before the landmark, 17 of 78 stage IV and 30 of 415 stage III TCGA patients had died, so the landmark sample was selected on survival more strongly in stage IV. When all four cohorts were used the stage IV coefficient was 0.00 (−0.56 to 0.51), and in the supermodel across landmarks stage IV carried a hazard ratio of 1.51 (1.12 to 2.03). Kaplan–Meier curves by progression history, the prespecified sensitivity analyses of the TCGA-only equations and subgroup hazard ratios are shown in Supplementary Figures S2 to S4.
P|Absolute risk transported poorly. The TCGA-only equation underpredicted mortality, with O/E ratios of 1.39 (1.04 to 1.74), 2.12 (1.56 to 2.76) and 1.26 (0.92 to 1.63). Internal–external fitting moved the ratios closer to one in AOCS (1.16) and ICON7 (0.96) but not in UKE (1.85), and overpredicted in TCGA (0.67). The pooled O/E was 1.08 with a 95% prediction interval from 0.15 to 7.67, and the pooled calibration slope was 1.09 (0.46 to 1.72) (Figure 2C and 2D). A cross-validated intercept update of the TCGA equation brought O/E to 1.00, 0.99 and 0.96 and reduced the Brier score from 0.131 to 0.122, 0.153 to 0.136 and 0.104 to 0.100 (Supplementary Table S8). Results changed little after multiple imputation of residual disease or after removal of the other German cohort from training (Supplementary Table S9). Each primary cohort contributed 29 to 50 deaths, whereas about 79 deaths are needed to estimate O/E within 0.8 to 1.2 and about 395 to estimate the calibration slope within ±0.1 (Supplementary Table S9).
H2|Why timing added nothing at month 12
P|At the month-12 landmark, 221 patients had progressed, and their median time since progression was 2.0 to 2.8 months across cohorts. Only 19 had progressed more than six months earlier (12 in TCGA, 5 in AOCS, 1 in UKE and 1 in ICON7). Patients who progress within the first six months and are still alive at month 12 are few and selected. The spread of the timing variable widened steadily with later landmarks: 77 of 295 progressed patients at month 15, 173 of 337 at month 18 and 235 of 339 at month 24 had progressed more than six months earlier (Figure 3A and Supplementary Table S10).
P|The association itself was present throughout. In the supermodel (929 patients contributing 5,534 patient-landmark records), each additional six months since progression was associated with a hazard ratio for death of 1.93 (1.53 to 2.43), and the single-landmark estimates were similar from month 12 (1.64; 0.91 to 2.95) to month 24 (1.74; 1.35 to 2.25) (Figure 3B). The pooled held-out AUC increment from timing was −0.000 at month 12, 0.016 (−0.009 to 0.042) at month 18, 0.061 (−0.002 to 0.124) at month 21 and 0.071 (0.006 to 0.136) at month 24. The increment from progression status was largest between months 12 and 18 (0.179 to 0.215) and smaller at month 6 (0.056) and month 24 (0.132) (Figure 3C and 3D).
H2|Progression timing and survival after progression
P|Following patients from first progression removed the restriction imposed by a single landmark. Of 1,220 eligible patients with recorded progression in the eight cohorts, 94 were excluded because the progression date coincided with death or last contact, leaving 1,126 patients and 726 deaths. Median post-progression survival rose from 11.0 months when progression occurred within six months of the time origin to 14.8, 22.1, 29.5 and 47.5 months for progression at 6 to under 12, 12 to under 18, 18 to under 24 and 24 or more months. The corresponding 12-month mortality after progression was 53.2%, 42.0%, 23.3%, 15.7% and 10.0% (Figure 4A and Supplementary Table S11).
P|In the primary cohorts (697 patients, 452 deaths) the hazard ratio per six months of later progression was 0.75 (0.70 to 0.81) after adjustment for age, stage and residual disease. Across all eight cohorts the two-stage random-effects estimate from patients with complete adjusters (1,061 patients, 682 deaths) was 0.73 (0.66 to 0.82), and a stage-adjusted one-stage model in all 1,126 patients gave 0.74 (0.70 to 0.78). The two-stage estimate had moderate heterogeneity (I², 35%) and a 95% prediction interval of 0.60 to 0.89 (Figure 4C). The relation was steeper for early than for late progression (test for nonlinearity, P < 0.001; Figure 4B) and stronger in the first 12 months after progression (0.62; 0.54 to 0.72) than later (0.81; 0.75 to 0.88). In held-out cohorts, adding time to progression to age, stage and residual disease raised concordance for post-progression survival by 0.088 (0.047 to 0.130) (Figure 4D). The illness–death model gave the same answer on the original time scale. Progression was associated with a hazard ratio for death of 22.1 (13.1 to 37.3), and among patients who had progressed each six months of later progression carried a hazard ratio of 0.71 (0.64 to 0.77). Table 3 summarises the timing estimates from all designs.
P|For a hypothetical trial with a 12-month mortality endpoint, 247 deaths would require about 1,416 unselected month-12 survivors, 546 patients with progression by month 12 or 3,169 patients without progression.
H2|Tumour expression, BRCA1/2 and progression
P|The T-cell score was associated with longer overall survival (pooled hazard ratio per SD, 0.88; 0.81 to 0.96), and the association persisted for death after the landmark with adjustment for progression status (0.86; 0.75 to 0.98; 1,333 patients, 378 deaths) and for post-progression survival with adjustment for time to progression (0.88; 0.80 to 0.97). The stromal score was associated with death after the landmark when adjusted for baseline factors (1.17; 1.02 to 1.35) and less clearly after adjustment for progression status (1.13; 0.99 to 1.28). The proliferation score showed no association (Figure 5A and Supplementary Table S12). None of the scores improved discrimination materially. Added to the Status model, the T-cell score changed the held-out AUC by 0.007 (−0.012 to 0.026) and all three scores together by 0.010 (−0.012 to 0.032) (Figure 5B).
P|Among 189 sequenced TCGA patients in the month-12 risk set, 43 carried a BRCA1/2 mutation (31 germline). Carrier status was not associated with death in the 24 months after the landmark (hazard ratio, 1.05; 0.55 to 2.01) but was associated with lower mortality over all follow-up (0.55; 0.35 to 0.86), without evidence of interaction with progression status (0.90; 0.31 to 2.61). Progression status remained strongly associated with death after adjustment for BRCA1/2 (2.64; 1.85 to 3.76). Adding BRCA1/2 to the Status model changed the out-of-fold AUC by 0.010 (−0.027 to 0.038) (Figure 5D and Supplementary Table S13).
P|HRD scores were available for 327 and CCNE1 copy number for 329 TCGA patients in the month-12 risk set. An HRD score of 42 or more was present in 184 patients and CCNE1 amplification in 62. The median HRD score was 58 in BRCA1/2 carriers and 38.5 in non-carriers, and 33 with and 49.5 without CCNE1 amplification. Progression by month 12 had been recorded in 22% of patients with a high and 40% with a low HRD score, and in 39% with and 28% without CCNE1 amplification. Both features behaved like BRCA1/2. With adjustment for progression status they were associated with death over all follow-up after the landmark (HRD score ≥42, hazard ratio 0.67; 0.51 to 0.89; CCNE1 amplification, 1.48; 1.07 to 2.05) but not with death in the first 24 months (0.91; 0.60 to 1.38 and 1.16; 0.72 to 1.86), and each changed the out-of-fold AUC by −0.006. A high HRD score was associated with longer post-progression survival after adjustment for time to progression (0.67; 0.50 to 0.90), whereas CCNE1 amplification was not (1.11; 0.78 to 1.57). In a joint model with BRCA1/2, HRD score and CCNE1 (178 patients), progression status kept a hazard ratio of 2.54 (1.74 to 3.70), and adding the three features changed the AUC by −0.007 (−0.053 to 0.038). Aneuploidy and fraction of genome altered showed no association, and a higher mutation burden was associated with lower mortality (Figure 6A and 6B and Supplementary Table S14).
P|Tumours that progressed within 12 months (301 in the primary and 144 in the additional cohorts) were compared with tumours progression-free at 12 months (690 and 381). Gene-level statistics were only weakly correlated between the two cohort sets (Spearman ρ, 0.22 across 8,814 genes), but hallmark enrichment scores agreed better (ρ, 0.33; P = 0.02). Five hallmarks reached a false discovery rate below 0.05 in the primary cohorts and three were replicated: epithelial–mesenchymal transition (normalised enrichment score 2.55 and 3.06), genes down-regulated by ultraviolet radiation (2.08 and 1.86) and angiogenesis (1.71 and 2.35). Lower oxidative phosphorylation and MYC target expression in early progressors was not replicated (Figure 5C and Supplementary Table S3).
P|Single-cell data indicated where these programmes are expressed. Of the expressed epithelial–mesenchymal transition hallmark genes, 62% had their highest expression in fibroblasts and 6% in malignant cells, compared with 16% and 28% of all genes (P < 0.001 for enrichment in fibroblasts). Of the 200 genes most increased in early-progressing tumours, 49% were highest in fibroblasts and 11% in malignant cells, and the 200 most decreased genes were enriched in malignant and T/NK cells. Across 5,678 genes the bulk statistic correlated positively with fibroblast (ρ, 0.22) and mesothelial (0.20) specificity and negatively with malignant (−0.12) and T/NK (−0.15) specificity. When the bulk comparison was adjusted for the stromal score, epithelial–mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis were no longer enriched (normalised enrichment score −1.34 and −1.05 in the primary cohorts). Instead, eight immune and inflammatory hallmarks were lower in early-progressing tumours in both cohort sets, including interferon-gamma response (−2.08 and −2.39) (Figure 6C and 6D and Supplementary Tables S15 and S16).
H1|Discussion
P|This study separates two statements that are easily confused. The first is that the timing of progression has little incremental predictive value at a month-12 visit. The second is that the timing of progression has little prognostic importance. Our data support the first and contradict the second. Across eight cohorts, each six months of later progression was associated with about a quarter lower hazard of death after progression, and the association was evident on three time scales and in every cohort. At month 12 this information is nearly invisible because almost all progressed patients have progressed within the preceding few months, and those who progressed earlier have mostly died before the visit. As the landmark moves later the range of the timing variable widens and its contribution to discrimination becomes measurable.
P|The finding places the month-12 landmark in its clinical context. With first-line chemotherapy ending roughly five to six months after diagnosis, progression recorded by month 12 corresponds broadly to relapse within six months of platinum, the group conventionally described as platinum resistant. The binary status at that visit therefore reproduces a familiar clinical category, which explains both its large effect and the small room left for a continuous timing term. Our results extend the pooled trial analysis of Su and colleagues [4] by quantifying discrimination and calibration in held-out cohorts, and they agree with the consensus view that the interval to relapse is a continuous prognostic variable without a natural threshold [2]. The time origins in these public data are diagnosis or randomisation, so the intervals are not platinum-free intervals and should not be read as such.
P|Three results bear on how such models should be built and reported. First, a model developed in one tissue-banking cohort gave an implausible negative coefficient for stage IV, the consequence of conditioning on survival to the landmark in a cohort with heavy early mortality in stage IV. Internal–external cross-validation removed that artefact and gave a more credible baseline comparator, against which progression status still added 0.18 to the AUC. Second, discrimination transported and probabilities did not. Prediction intervals for the O/E ratio were too wide to support use of an unadjusted equation in a new centre, and a simple intercept update within each cohort corrected most of the error. Third, every external cohort had fewer than the roughly 80 to 400 events needed for precise calibration estimates, so cohort-specific calibration results are imprecise and should be read with the pooled intervals.
P|The molecular analyses lead to a similar distinction between association and incremental value. A T-cell expression score measured at diagnosis remained associated with death after progression status was known, consistent with the established prognostic role of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes [41], and BRCA1/2 mutation and a high HRD score were associated with lower, and CCNE1 amplification with higher, long-term mortality after the landmark, in line with the survival pattern of carriers and the link between CCNE1 amplification and primary resistance [42, 43]. None was associated with death in the next 24 months or changed the AUC by more than 0.02. Early progression was accompanied by mesenchymal and angiogenic expression programmes in two independent sets of cohorts, which matches the poor outcome of the mesenchymal expression subtype [11, 32], whereas single-gene differences were not reproducible. The single-cell and stroma-adjusted analyses argue against reading this signal as epithelial–mesenchymal transition of cancer cells. The genes are expressed mainly by fibroblasts, the enrichment disappears once stromal content is accounted for, and what remains is lower immune and interferon signalling, which agrees with the T-cell score. For short-term prediction at a follow-up visit, the observed clinical course outweighed the baseline expression features available here.
P|For clinical use, these results support progression status at a fixed visit as a stratification or enrichment variable in studies of post-progression treatment. A trial with a 12-month mortality endpoint would need about 40% as many patients if restricted to those with progression by month 12. The results do not support individual risk communication, earlier imaging or earlier treatment on the basis of the predicted probability. A randomised trial of early treatment triggered by rising CA-125 showed that detecting relapse earlier does not by itself improve survival [44]. Any clinical tool would need development in contemporary cohorts with BRCA and homologous recombination status, maintenance therapy and performance status, with a defined decision and local recalibration [3, 24].
P|The study has limitations. The cohorts are historical, selected for tissue availability and treated before maintenance PARP inhibitors, so absolute risks do not apply to current practice. Progression definitions, assessment schedules and time origins differed between sources, and TCGA progression-free interval events can include new tumour events that are not labelled as recurrence. Histological type and grade were not centrally reviewed, and high-grade serous carcinoma could not be defined uniformly [45]. Overlap between UKE, OVCAD and the ICON7 subset cannot be excluded, although results were unchanged when the other German cohort was removed from training. Treatment after progression was not recorded. Race and ethnicity were unavailable in most sources, so performance across such groups could not be examined. All analyses other than the TCGA-locked comparison were post hoc, the gene sets were chosen by the authors and the molecular results are exploratory. Genomic features were available only in TCGA. The single-cell data come from 11 patients, mostly from metastatic sites, without outcome data, and contained too few endothelial cells to assess the origin of the angiogenesis signal. The multiple-imputation analysis varied only residual disease. Finally, the post-progression analyses condition on a recorded progression and describe association, not the effect of delaying progression.
H1|Conclusions
P|In advanced serous tubo-ovarian carcinoma the timing of first progression is strongly associated with survival after progression, but at a month-12 visit nearly all of the usable prognostic information is contained in whether progression has occurred. The value of timing for prediction grows as follow-up lengthens. Models built this way rank patients consistently across cohorts and need recalibration before their probabilities are used in a new population.
